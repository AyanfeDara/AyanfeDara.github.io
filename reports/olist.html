Olist e-commerce: growth came from volume, and late deliveries drive 1-star reviews

Orders delivered more than a week late received a 1-star review 70% of the time. Late orders were 6.7% of deliveries but produced 37% of all 1-star reviews.

Tools
PostgreSQL, SQL (CTEs, window functions, conditional aggregation), Python (pandas, matplotlib)
Data
Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist, 99,441 orders across 9 linked tables, Sep 2016 to Oct 2018
Code on GitHub ↗

Business question

Olist's management wants to know what drove revenue growth between 2017 and 2018, whether customers come back, and whether delivery problems are hurting customer ratings.

Findings

1. Revenue grew 141%, and the growth came from more orders, not bigger orders

Revenue from delivered orders rose from BRL 2.99M (Jan to Aug 2017) to BRL 7.22M (Jan to Aug 2018). Orders grew from 22,000 to 52,800 over the same months, while the average order value stayed flat at about BRL 136. The November 2017 spike matches Black Friday.

Show the SQL
-- Q1. What drove Olist's revenue growth from 2017 to 2018?
-- Why it matters:
--  Management wants to know whether growth came from more orders,
--  bigger orders, or a few categories.

-- Part A: monthly revenue, order count and average order value
WITH monthly AS (
    SELECT DATE_TRUNC('month', o.order_purchase_timestamp)::date AS month,
           COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id) AS orders,
           SUM(i.price) AS revenue
    FROM orders o
    JOIN order_items i ON i.order_id = o.order_id
    WHERE o.order_status = 'delivered'
      AND o.order_purchase_timestamp >= '2017-01-01'
      AND o.order_purchase_timestamp <  '2018-09-01'
    GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT month,
       orders,
       revenue,
       ROUND(revenue / orders, 2) AS avg_order_value,
       ROUND(100.0 * (revenue - LAG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY month))
             / LAG(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY month), 1) AS revenue_mom_change_pct
FROM monthly
ORDER BY month;



-- Part B: top 5 categories by revenue growth (2018 Jan-Aug against 2017 Jan-Aug)
WITH category_revenue AS (
    SELECT COALESCE(t.product_category_name_english, p.product_category_name, 'unknown') AS category,
           COALESCE(SUM(i.price) FILTER (WHERE o.order_purchase_timestamp >= '2017-01-01'
                                           AND o.order_purchase_timestamp <  '2017-09-01'), 0) AS revenue_2017,
           COALESCE(SUM(i.price) FILTER (WHERE o.order_purchase_timestamp >= '2018-01-01'
                                           AND o.order_purchase_timestamp <  '2018-09-01'), 0) AS revenue_2018
    FROM orders o
    JOIN order_items i ON i.order_id = o.order_id
    JOIN products p ON p.product_id = i.product_id
    LEFT JOIN category_translation t ON t.product_category_name = p.product_category_name
    WHERE o.order_status = 'delivered'
    GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT category,
       revenue_2017,
       revenue_2018,
       revenue_2018 - revenue_2017 AS growth_abs,
       ROUND(100.0 * (revenue_2018 - revenue_2017) / NULLIF(revenue_2017, 0), 1) AS growth_pct
FROM category_revenue
ORDER BY growth_abs DESC
LIMIT 5;

2. Five categories delivered 44% of the growth

Health & beauty added the most revenue (+BRL 512K). Watches & gifts grew fastest, more than tripling (+242%). Together the top five categories added BRL 1.86M of the BRL 4.22M increase.

3. Only 3% of customers ever come back

Of 94,990 customers, 2,888 (3.04%) placed a second order. The median time to the second order was 28 days. Of those repeat customers, 30% ordered again on the same day, which looks more like one shopping trip split into two orders than a return visit, so true loyalty is lower still (about 2.1%).

Show the SQL
-- Q2. How many customers come back, and how fast?
-- Why it matters:
--  A business that relies only on first-time buyers spends heavily on
--  acquisition. The repeat rate shows how much room retention has.

-- Part A: repeat purchase rate
WITH customer_orders AS (
    SELECT c.customer_unique_id,
           COUNT(DISTINCT o.order_id) AS orders
    FROM orders o
    JOIN customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
    WHERE o.order_status NOT IN ('canceled', 'unavailable')
    GROUP BY c.customer_unique_id
)
SELECT COUNT(*) AS customers,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE orders >= 2) AS repeat_customers,
       ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE orders >= 2) / COUNT(*), 2) AS repeat_rate_pct
FROM customer_orders;


-- Part B: days between first and second order for repeat customers
WITH ranked AS (
    SELECT c.customer_unique_id,
           o.order_purchase_timestamp,
           ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY c.customer_unique_id
                              ORDER BY o.order_purchase_timestamp, o.order_id) AS order_number
    FROM orders o
    JOIN customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
    WHERE o.order_status NOT IN ('canceled', 'unavailable')
),
first_two AS (
    SELECT customer_unique_id,
           MAX(order_purchase_timestamp) FILTER (WHERE order_number = 1)::date AS first_order,
           MAX(order_purchase_timestamp) FILTER (WHERE order_number = 2)::date AS second_order
    FROM ranked
    WHERE order_number <= 2
    GROUP BY customer_unique_id
    HAVING COUNT(*) = 2
)
SELECT COUNT(*) AS repeat_customers,
       PERCENTILE_CONT(0.5) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY second_order - first_order) AS median_days,
       ROUND(AVG(second_order - first_order), 1) AS avg_days,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE second_order = first_order) AS same_day,
       ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE second_order = first_order) / COUNT(*), 1) AS same_day_pct
FROM first_two;

4. Late deliveries go with bad reviews

Orders that arrived more than a week early averaged 4.32 stars; orders more than a week late averaged 1.70, and 70% of them received 1 star. Only 6.7% of delivered orders were late, yet they account for 37% of all 1-star reviews.

Show the SQL
-- Q3. Do late deliveries cause bad reviews?
-- Why it matters:
--  If lateness drives low scores, fixing logistics protects ratings,
--  which drive future sales on the marketplace.

-- Part A: average review score by delay bucket
WITH order_scores AS (
    SELECT order_id,
           AVG(review_score) AS review_score
    FROM order_reviews
    GROUP BY order_id
),
delivered AS (
    SELECT o.order_id,
           o.order_delivered_customer_date::date - o.order_estimated_delivery_date::date AS delay_days
    FROM orders o
    WHERE o.order_status = 'delivered'
      AND o.order_delivered_customer_date IS NOT NULL
),
bucketed AS (
    SELECT d.order_id,
           s.review_score,
           CASE
               WHEN d.delay_days < -7 THEN 1
               WHEN d.delay_days < 0  THEN 2
               WHEN d.delay_days = 0  THEN 3
               WHEN d.delay_days <= 3 THEN 4
               WHEN d.delay_days <= 7 THEN 5
               ELSE 6
           END AS sort_key
    FROM delivered d
    JOIN order_scores s ON s.order_id = d.order_id
)
SELECT sort_key,
       CASE sort_key
           WHEN 1 THEN 'More than 7 days early'
           WHEN 2 THEN '1-7 days early'
           WHEN 3 THEN 'On the estimated day'
           WHEN 4 THEN '1-3 days late'
           WHEN 5 THEN '4-7 days late'
           ELSE 'More than 7 days late'
       END AS delay_bucket,
       COUNT(*) AS orders,
       ROUND(AVG(review_score), 2) AS avg_review_score,
       ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE review_score = 1) / COUNT(*), 1) AS pct_1_star
FROM bucketed
GROUP BY sort_key
ORDER BY sort_key;


-- Part B: share of all 1-star reviews that come from late orders
WITH order_scores AS (
    SELECT order_id,
           AVG(review_score) AS review_score
    FROM order_reviews
    GROUP BY order_id
),
delivered AS (
    SELECT o.order_id,
           o.order_delivered_customer_date::date - o.order_estimated_delivery_date::date AS delay_days
    FROM orders o
    WHERE o.order_status = 'delivered'
      AND o.order_delivered_customer_date IS NOT NULL
)
SELECT COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE s.review_score = 1) AS one_star_reviews,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE s.review_score = 1 AND d.delay_days > 0) AS one_star_from_late,
       ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE s.review_score = 1 AND d.delay_days > 0)
                   / COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE s.review_score = 1), 1) AS pct_from_late,
       ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE d.delay_days > 0) / COUNT(*), 1) AS pct_of_orders_late
FROM delivered d
JOIN order_scores s ON s.order_id = d.order_id;

5. The North-east has the worst delivery performance

The five states with the highest late-delivery rates (AL, MA, SE, PI, CE) are all in the North-east, at 14 to 21% late against 4.5% in São Paulo. Delivery to Alagoas took 24.5 days on average, almost three times São Paulo's 8.7 days, and these states also give lower review scores.

Show the SQL
-- Q4. Where are deliveries slowest, and does that line up with lower scores?
-- Why it matters:
--  Shows logistics where to invest first.

-- Part A: delivery performance by state
WITH order_scores AS (
    SELECT order_id,
           AVG(review_score) AS review_score
    FROM order_reviews
    GROUP BY order_id
)
SELECT c.customer_state,
       COUNT(*) AS orders,
       ROUND(AVG(o.order_delivered_customer_date::date - o.order_purchase_timestamp::date), 1) AS avg_delivery_days,
       ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE o.order_delivered_customer_date::date
                                          > o.order_estimated_delivery_date::date) / COUNT(*), 1) AS late_rate_pct,
       ROUND(AVG(s.review_score), 2) AS avg_review_score
FROM orders o
JOIN customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
LEFT JOIN order_scores s ON s.order_id = o.order_id
WHERE o.order_status = 'delivered'
  AND o.order_delivered_customer_date IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY c.customer_state
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 300
ORDER BY late_rate_pct DESC;


-- Part B: rank states
WITH order_scores AS (
    SELECT order_id,
           AVG(review_score) AS review_score
    FROM order_reviews
    GROUP BY order_id
),
state_performance AS (
    SELECT c.customer_state,
           COUNT(*) AS orders,
           ROUND(AVG(o.order_delivered_customer_date::date - o.order_purchase_timestamp::date), 1) AS avg_delivery_days,
           ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE o.order_delivered_customer_date::date
                                              > o.order_estimated_delivery_date::date) / COUNT(*), 1) AS late_rate_pct,
           ROUND(AVG(s.review_score), 2) AS avg_review_score
    FROM orders o
    JOIN customers c ON c.customer_id = o.customer_id
    LEFT JOIN order_scores s ON s.order_id = o.order_id
    WHERE o.order_status = 'delivered'
      AND o.order_delivered_customer_date IS NOT NULL
    GROUP BY c.customer_state
    HAVING COUNT(*) >= 300
),
ranked AS (
    SELECT *,
           RANK() OVER (ORDER BY late_rate_pct DESC) AS late_rank
    FROM state_performance
)
SELECT late_rank, customer_state, orders, avg_delivery_days, late_rate_pct, avg_review_score
FROM ranked
WHERE late_rank <= 5
ORDER BY late_rank;


-- Part C

Recommendations

  • Fix delivery to the North-east first. Review carrier performance and delivery estimates for AL, MA, SE, PI and CE, where late rates are three to five times São Paulo's.
  • Act on orders at risk of running late. Orders more than a week late get 1 star 70% of the time, so flag delayed orders early and set more realistic estimates on slow routes.
  • Test a second-order incentive. With 97% of customers buying once, a follow-up offer timed around four weeks after the first purchase (the median return time) is a low-cost experiment.
  • Back the growth categories. Health & beauty and watches & gifts drove a quarter of all growth; seller recruitment and promotion there has the clearest payoff.

Data and method

  • Source: nine linked tables loaded into PostgreSQL (sql/00_create_schema.sql, sql/01_load_data.sql).
  • Data quality checks: sql/02_data_checks.sql, with findings and decisions in notes/data_quality.md.
  • Key decisions:
  • Revenue counts delivered orders only (97% of orders) and uses item price, excluding freight.
  • Year-on-year comparisons use Jan to Aug of each year, because 2016 and Sep to Oct 2018 are incomplete.
  • Review scores are averaged per order first, so the 547 orders with several reviews are not counted twice.
  • Repeat customers are counted with customer_unique_id, since Olist creates a new customer_id for every order.
  • State results include only states with at least 300 delivered orders (21 of 27).
  • Queries: one file per question in sql/ (10_q1 to 13_q4); results in results/; charts made with charts/make_charts.py.

Limitations

  • The data covers one Brazilian marketplace from 2016 to 2018, so patterns may differ elsewhere or today.
  • Delivery delays and low scores are associated, which does not prove that lateness causes them; product quality and seller behaviour could also play a part.
  • Revenue excludes freight and instalment charges, so it measures product sales rather than cash collected.
